Cost accounting

Cost accounting (Kostenrechnung) is where most candidates fail. It is rarely the maths, but the structure: costs put in the wrong place, owner's salary forgotten, commission calculated wrongly. In this lesson you learn a fixed scheme that you can always apply the same way in the exam.

Exam area 2.2 · Cost accountingapprox. 50 minutesPBZugV Annex 3 No. 2.3DIHK syllabus 2.2Deutsche Version

After this lesson you can

  • reliably split costs into fixed and variable
  • calculate depreciation and imputed interest
  • calculate costs per vehicle, per km, per occupied km and per trip
  • determine contribution margin and break-even point
  • correctly calculate the revenue needed with a platform commission
  • distinguish cost type, cost centre and cost unit accounting

Why cost accounting?

As an operator you must know what one kilometre costs you. Only then do you know whether a trip pays off, whether the taxi tariff covers your costs and what price to charge for a Mietwagen trip.

The exam almost always has a larger calculation task. It usually follows the same order:

  1. Collect the fixed costs (Fixkosten) per year, including depreciation, interest and owner's salary.
  2. Calculate the variable costs (variable Kosten) per kilometre.
  3. Total costs per year = fixed costs + variable costs × kilometres.
  4. Costs per unit: per km, per occupied km, per trip.
  5. From this derive the price, revenue or break-even point.
Important

In cost accounting always calculate with net amounts, i.e. without VAT. For you VAT is a pass-through item. More in lesson 1.10.

Step 1: fixed or variable?

Fixed costs arise even if the car stays in the garage. Variable costs only arise with every kilometre driven or every euro of revenue.

Fixed costs (per year)Variable costs (per km or % of revenue)
Time-based depreciationFuel or electricity

Motor liability and comprehensive insuranceMaintenance and repair by km

Rule of thumb

Ask yourself: “Do I pay this even if I don't drive at all for a month?” Yes = fixed. No = variable.

Step 2: depreciation and imputed interest

Depreciation

Your car loses value every year. You spread this loss of value as depreciation (Abschreibung) over the years of its useful life. In cost accounting you first deduct the residual value, i.e. the price you still get when selling.

Depreciation per year = (purchase price − residual value) ÷ useful life
Example (sample values)

Purchase price 42.000 €, residual value 12.000 €, useful life 4 years.
(42.000 − 12.000) ÷ 4 = 7.500 € per year

There are two kinds:

  • Time-based depreciation (per year): part of the fixed costs. This is the normal case in exam tasks.
  • Usage-based depreciation (per km): part of the variable costs. Formula: (purchase price − residual value) ÷ total mileage in km.

Read the task carefully. If it gives a mileage (“the vehicle should run 300.000 km”), usage-based depreciation is often meant.

Cost accounting is not tax

For tax purposes you depreciate a car according to the official depreciation table, without residual value. In cost accounting you use the real useful life and the residual value. The task tells you which is meant.

Imputed interest

You could also put the money tied up in the car in the bank. You include this lost interest as imputed interest (kalkulatorische Zinsen), even if you have no loan. Because the car is worth less each year, you use the average capital tied up:

Imputed interest = (purchase price + residual value) ÷ 2 × interest rate
Example (sample values)

(42.000 + 12.000) ÷ 2 × 5 % = 27.000 × 0,05 = 1.350 € per year

Even if you have a loan, you usually use imputed interest in cost accounting, not the actual loan interest. Otherwise you would count twice. Always follow the task.

Step 3: don't forget the owner's salary

If you drive yourself, the business pays you no wage. Still your work costs something. That is why you include an imputed owner's salary (kalkulatorischer Unternehmerlohn). It equals the salary you would get as an employee for the same work.

Most common mistake

Forgetting the owner's salary makes your costs too low. The price then covers the car, but not your work. In the exam this costs points.

Step 4: costs per km, per occupied km and per trip

Total costs = fixed costs + variable costs per km × km driven
Cost per km = total costs ÷ km driven
Cost per occupied km = total costs ÷ (km driven × occupied share)
Cost per trip = total costs ÷ number of trips

A taxi does not always have a passenger. The drive to the customer and the return to the rank or the office are empty kilometres. Only the occupied kilometres (Besetztkilometer) are paid. So the occupied kilometres must carry all costs.

Remember

The cost per occupied km is the figure that decides the price. It is always higher than the cost per km driven.

Contribution margin and break-even point

The contribution margin (Deckungsbeitrag) shows how much of every euro of revenue is left to pay the fixed costs.

Contribution margin = revenue − variable costs

As long as the contribution margin is positive, every trip helps to cover the fixed costs. Once all fixed costs are covered, profit begins. This point is called the break-even point (Gewinnschwelle).

Break-even in km = fixed costs ÷ contribution margin per km
Example (sample values)

Fixed costs 52.230 € per year. Variable costs 0,16875 € per km driven. Revenue 2,20 € per occupied km. Occupied share 55 %.

Revenue per km driven = 2,20 € × 55 % = 1,2100 € Contribution margin per km = 1,2100 € − 0,16875 € = 1,04125 € Break-even = 52.230 € ÷ 1,04125 €/km ≈ 50.161 km From about 50.161 km driven per year the business makes a profit.
What is it for?
  • Is a trip at a low fixed price worth it? Yes, if the price covers at least the variable costs, as long as the fixed costs are covered anyway. In the long run the price must cover all costs.
  • How many km must I drive so that I don't make a loss?

Mietwagen with an app: calculating commission correctly

Platforms keep a commission (Provision) of the revenue. If the app keeps 25 %, you get only 75 € out of 100 € revenue. So you need more revenue to cover your costs.

Revenue needed = costs ÷ (1 − commission rate)
Example (sample values)
Costs: 60.000 € Commission: 25 % Correct: 60.000 € ÷ (1 − 0,25) = 60.000 € ÷ 0,75 = 80.000 € Check: 80.000 € − 25 % (20.000 €) = 60.000 € ✓ Wrong: 60.000 € × 1,25 = 75.000 € Check: 75.000 € − 25 % (18.750 €) = 56.250 € ✗ too little
Typical mistake

The commission is deducted from revenue, not from costs. So you divide by (1 − rate) and don't multiply by (1 + rate). Always do the check.

Cost types, cost centres, cost units

The syllabus names three stages of cost accounting. You should be able to tell them apart:

StageQuestionExample in a taxi business
Cost type accounting KostenartenrechnungWhich costs arose?fuel, insurance, wages, depreciation
Cost centre accounting KostenstellenrechnungWhere did they arise?vehicle 1, vehicle 2, office / dispatch
Cost unit accounting KostenträgerrechnungWhat for did they arise?the single trip, a booking, a kilometre

One also distinguishes direct costs (Einzelkosten) and overheads (Gemeinkosten). Direct costs can be assigned directly to a vehicle or a trip, for example the fuel of taxi 1. Overheads concern several, for example the office rent. They are distributed with a key, for example by number of vehicles or by km.

Typical mistakes

!Fuel entered as a fixed cost.Fuel is variable. It depends on the km driven.
!Owner's salary forgotten.Always include it as a fixed cost if you work in the business yourself.
!Interest calculated on the full purchase price.(Purchase price + residual value) ÷ 2 × interest rate.
!Cost per km confused with cost per occupied km.For the price, the occupied km count.
!Commission calculated with × (1 + rate).Revenue = costs ÷ (1 − rate).
!Units mixed up: cent and euro, litres per 100 km and per km.Write the unit next to every number.
!Calculated with gross amounts.Cost accounting is always net.
